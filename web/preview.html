Sample lines per side. At 136 the formula is evaluated 136×136 times and the surface is built from that grid: higher means finer detail and a slower rebuild, nothing else.
The shape of the box the surface is drawn in, not the values it holds. Raise the height to see shallow ripples that the default 0.3 flattens; even makes it a plain cube. With the height camera there is no box to shape — the third number becomes vertical exaggeration there, and 0.3 means the proportions frozen with the formula.
Off by default on purpose: an automatic refit makes the geometry a consequence of the camera, and the relief sinks the moment a new peak comes into view. Switched on, it refits only when the range needed is more than twice the one in use, and says so when it does.
The vertical scale and the colour ramp are one thing, fixed when you change the formula and left alone by the camera — otherwise moving the view would change the shape of the plot. Press this to refit both to the region you are looking at now.

Nashira3D is a three‑dimensional plotting library for Python. It is built for pictures that are exact, predictable, and worth looking at — surfaces today; volumes, vector fields, and point clouds in time. The idea behind it is short: the hard part belongs inside the library, and what opens in front of you is space.

the name

Nashira is the name the International Astronomical Union keeps on its list for γ Capricorni. Traditionally it means the fortunate one, or the one who brings good news.

I took it for the sound first — it is rare, and it is beautiful. Then it turned out to say what the project is. A point of light fixes a position in space. It gives depth to what would otherwise be flat. It turns darkness into a scene you can read.

where it came from

I wanted three‑dimensional graphics in Python without the usual trade. The tools I had kept asking me to choose: simple or fast, handsome or portable, an interactive window or a file written on a machine with no screen at all. I wanted one way of working that holds in all three places.

what it grew out of

No single program. What pulls me is the task itself — turning numbers into space. A function becomes a surface, a column of values becomes relief, a scatter of points becomes a shape, and a calculation becomes something you can walk around and understand.

still an experiment

Version 0.2 draws one thing: a surface z = f(x, y). That is where it starts, not where it stops. Volumes, vector fields, point clouds, and fuller scenes belong here as well, and the interface was written so they can arrive without breaking what already works — it asks you for quality, not for a grid size.

So take this as an experiment for now. The work is honest and the ground under it is solid, but it is not finished, and it will keep changing.

Drag to circle the plot. The plot is drawn inside a cube, and the left button circles it. The wheel moves the camera closer and further; double‑click on the surface to say what the orbit should turn around. Shift or the right button moves the view, R resets it, and the switch below turns the cube into an endless plane you stand on.
